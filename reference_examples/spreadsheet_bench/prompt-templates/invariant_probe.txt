## What the INVARIANTS declaration means
Before accepting your solution I will re-run **your exact program** on modified copies of
`input.xlsx` and compare only the `answer_position` cells against the unmodified run. Each
line is your prediction of that comparison:

- `DEPENDS:` — the sheets whose cell values your answer genuinely reads. If I modify a
  sheet you did not list and your answer moves, your program is reading something the
  task never asked about.
- `DUP_ROW:` — `same` if appending a duplicate of an existing data row must leave your
  answer unchanged (lookups, de-duplication, distinct counts, min/max, sorting);
  `different` if your answer legitimately counts, sums or averages rows.
- `ROW_ORDER:` — `same` if reversing the order of the data rows must leave your answer
  unchanged (sorting, aggregation, lookup by key, set operations); `different` only if
  the task is genuinely about the incoming row order.
- `CASE:` — `same` if flipping the letter-case of the text in one sheet must leave your
  answer unchanged. Note that Excel's own lookup and comparison functions (MATCH, VLOOKUP,
  COUNTIF, `=`) are case-INSENSITIVE, whereas Python's `==`, `in` and `dict` keys are
  case-SENSITIVE — so a literal Python port of a spreadsheet operation often disagrees
  with what the task actually requires.

Predict what the **task** requires, not what your current code happens to do. These are
also the cases the hidden inputs will exercise: duplicate keys, a different row order and
different capitalisation are exactly what distinguishes a general program from one tuned
to the rows in front of you. If the check disagrees with your prediction, one of the two
is wrong and you will be given the chance to fix it.
